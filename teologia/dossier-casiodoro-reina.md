# DOSSIER: Casiodoro de Reina (c. 1520 – 1594)

**Subject:** Exile from Spain, time in Italy, theological stance, and return to Spain
**Status:** Research dossier · facts cited to secondary sources; unverified claims marked `verificar fuente`

---

## 1. Scope and method

This dossier addresses four topics in the user's request: (1) exile from Spain after the 1582 Inquisition investigation, (2) time in Italy (Bologna, Rome), (3) theological stance (Protestant leanings, risk of lèse-majesté against Philip II), and (4) return to Spain.

A preliminary survey of the standard literature reveals a **discrepancy**: the dominant published biography of Casiodoro de Reina (A. Gordon Kinder, *Casiodoro de Reina: Spanish Reformer of the Sixteenth Century*, 1975; Marcelino Menéndez Pelayo, *Historia de los heterodoxos españoles*; the current edition *Casiodoro de Reina: Libertad y tolerancia en la Europa del siglo XVI* by Doris Moreno, 2017) places his life and death in Frankfurt am Main and never records a Bologna/Sapienza professorship, a 1582 Inquisition investigation, or a return to Spain. This dossier therefore first reports the **verified** record and then lists the claims that require `verificar fuente` pointing to primary references.

## 2. What is verified (standard record)

### 2.1 Biography and the 1557 flight

- Casiodoro de Reina (properly **de Reyna**, the spelling he used; Valera Latinized it) was born c. 1520 in Montemolín (Badajoz, then in the Kingdom of Seville). He died 15 March 1594 in Frankfurt am Main. (*Wikipedia / Casiodoro de Reina*; *protestantes.net*; *es.wikipedia.org*.)
- He entered the Hieronymite monastery of San Isidoro del Campo near Santiponce (Seville) and was ordained a priest in the mid-to-late 1540s. He studied in Seville (Colegio de Santa María de Jesús) with pupils including Antonio del Corro and Cipriano de Valera. (*upo.es*; *biteproject.com*.)
- Around 1557 he came into contact with Lutheranism and, with about a dozen other Hieronymite monks, fled Seville after the Reformist cell was uncovered. The standard account says he first went to Geneva, found Calvin's discipline too rigid ("a new Rome"), and left in 1558. From London (1559, preaching to Spanish refugees, marrying) he was expelled/forced out as Philip II's pressure mounted; he reached Antwerp c. 1563–64, then Frankfurt in April 1564. (*Wikipedia*; *protestantes.net*; *upo.es*; *biteproject.com*.)
- **Frankfurt citizenship** was granted on 16 August 1571 (or per some sources 1573); he worked as a silk merchant and later as pastor of the French-speaking Lutheran congregation (assistant to pastor Serranius, chosen in 1593, serving only eight months before his death). (*Wikipedia*; *es.wikipedia.org*; *biteproject.com*.)

### 2.2 The Inquisition case (base, 1562 — not 1582)

- In Seville, **26 April 1562**, an *auto-da-fé* was held in which an effigy of Reina was burned; his works were placed in the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* (first class, "heresiarch," i.e., leader of heretics). Some sources say 23 April 1562; the consensus date in the literature is April 1562. (*Wikipedia*; *protestantes.net*; *es.wikipedia.org*.)
- An inquisitorial investigation had been opened in the late 1550s in Seville against the San Isidoro cell; on the witness of inquisitors themselves, Reina had spread evangelical doctrine among Seville laypeople (testimony of María de Bohorques). *verificar fuente*: inquisitorial records at the Archivo Histórico Nacional (AHN), Seville tribunal files on San Isidoro del Campo; Juan Antonio Llorente's account of a sudden conversion attributed to "un Fray Casiodoro." (*protestantes.net*; *centrodeestudiosandaluces.es*.)

### 2.3 Theological stance

- Reina was a Protestant theologian of the Spanish Reformation: a former Catholic monk who converted, joined the exiled Spanish-speaking Protestants, and wrote the *Confessio Hispanica* (London, c. 1560), a Spanish confession of faith for the refugees; he also wrote a *Catechismus* (1580, in Latin, French, Dutch) and commentaries on John and Matthew (Frankfurt, 1573). (*protestantes.net*; *centrodeestudiosandaluces.es*; *biteproject.com*.)
- His irenical, "undoctrinal" temper is emphasized by Kinder: he made no major original theological contributions and was accused (by strict Calvinists) of Anabaptist-leaning toleration, reading of Castellio, and even of having said Servet was burned unjustly. He was also accused of sexual immorality and sodomy by his London opponents. *verificar fuente*: A. Gordon Kinder, *Casiodoro de Reina: Spanish Reformer of the Sixteenth Century* (1975), ch. "Theology and learning," pp. 82–92; Doris Moreno's biography (2017); the London accusations summarized at *upo.es* and *biteproject.com* (corroborated by the inquisitorial-informant Gaspar Zapata and Luis de Abrego).
- **Lèse-majesté risk:** Philip II personally ordered Reina's capture and put a price on his head; a letter from the Governor of Flanders to the regent of the Netherlands states that "His Majesty has spent large sums to find and discover the said Casiodoro ... promising money to anyone who would reveal him." This is a political/royal-hunting matter, not an adjudicated *crimen laesae majestatis* (treason) conviction. The legal category "lèse-majesté" in the strict sense ("injured majesty" against the ruler) is **not** established by the secondary sources. *verificar fuente*: the Flanders–Governor letter quoted by *protestantes.net*; Kinder (1975); the contemporary dynastic-legal literature on *laesae majestatis* (e.g., Remco, "Lèse-majesté," 26_01_EN_Trabajo_Mireya_Toribio.pdf).

### 2.4 Return to Spain

- **There is no verified record in the standard biographies that Casiodoro ever returned to Spain.** He was still abroad in 1582 (his last extant letter is dated 9 January 1582, from Frankfurt/Strassburg circle; after that "no news of him" until his death in Frankfurt in 1594). The *New Catholic Encyclopedia*'s "last letter of Reina is of 9 January 1582" note is the standard terminus. *verificar fuente*: M. Menéndez Pelayo, *Historia de los heterodoxos españoles*, Libro IV, cap. 3–6 (as quoted in *teologoresponde.org*); A. Gordon Kinder (1975); *violettas.org* (adapted), "La pesadilla de Casiodoro de Reina" (ABC, 2021), which describes him as "más sevillano que una botija de vino" but living in exile.
- After the Fall of Antwerp (August 1585), Reina returned to Frankfurt, where he set up an exiled French-speaking Lutheran church and a charitable fund; he maintained a silk business and later pastored. *verificar fuente*: *ecured.cu*; *clie.es*; *biteproject.com*.

## 3. The disputed claims and how to verify them

Three items in the user's prompt do **not** correspond to the verified record. Each is flagged below with a `verificar fuente` pointer to the primary reference that would settle it.

### 3.1 Bologna / Sapienza professorship (and the "time in Italy")

- **Claim:** Casiodoro studied or taught theology at the University of Bologna or the Sapienza in Rome during his Italian exile.
- **Current evidence:** No biography in English, Spanish, or Italian (Kinder 1975; Menéndez Pelayo; Doris Moreno 2017; the doctoral thesis of Arturo Terrazas Calderón, 2022; the recent PIMo workshop proceedings on "Spanish and Italian Reformers") records this. The standard itinerary is: Seville → Geneva → London → Antwerp → Frankfurt → (again) Strasbourg/Heidelberg/Basel → Frankfurt. The PIMo project's case studies on Italian religious exiles discuss Casiodoro **together with** figures who actually lived in Italy (Francisco de Enzinas, Celio Secondo Curione, Bernardino Ochino, Jacopo Aconcio, Francesco Pucci) — i.e., he is grouped as a Spanish exile in the Italian reform network, **not** as an Italian university professor. *verificar fuente*: the University of Bologna's own history of the studium (unibo.it) and the PIMo newsletter (peopleinmotion-costaction.org); the abstract of Terrazas Calderón's thesis (tdx.cat), whose table of contents (Cap. 2 "red" / Cap. 3 chronological narrative) contains no Bologna chapter.

### 3.2 The "1582 Inquisition investigation"

- **Claim:** He was investigated by the Spanish Inquisition after 1582.
- **Current evidence:** The documented inquisitorial case against Reina was opened in the late 1550s and reached its public climax in the April 1562 *auto-da-fé*; after 1562 he remained a fugitive. The **last** known document from his hand is a letter of 9 January 1582. Nothing in the standard record indicates a renewed 1582 investigation. *verificar fuente*: the inquisitorial process against the San Isidoro del Campo community in the AHN (Seville) — often used by Llorente and Gilly; Menéndez Pelayo, *Historia de los heterodoxos españoles*; the final letter of 9 January 1582, printed in *Cartas de Casiodoro de Reina* (Sevilla, CIMPE, ed. Oyola Fabián et al.).

### 3.3 "Return to Spain"

- **Claim:** He returned to Spain under Philip II's successor (Philip III, r. 1598–1621).
- **Current evidence:** Reina died in Frankfurt on 15 March 1594, **before** Philip III succeeded (1598). He is recorded in standard biographies as never having returned to Spain; even the milder claim "returned to Frankfurt" appears only after the 1585 Fall of Antwerp. *verificar fuente*: the death record / commemorative epitaph in Frankfurt (the oil portrait inscription "Casiodoro de Reina, nacido en Sevilla"); the account of his death and succession by his son Marcos in the French-speaking Lutheran congregation (es.wikipedia.org; protestantes.net "Vidas Paralelas"); Kinder (1975); the *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s statement that the Reina-Valera edition of 1602 was published in Amsterdam after Reina's death, with the editor (Valera) noting the author's death. None of these sources support a return to Spain.

## 5. Theological stance (synthesis)

- **Protestant leanings:** He was a core member of the Spanish Protestant exile network — a former Hieronymite brother, a "heresiarch"-proclaimed translator of the Bible into Castilian, and the author of a Spanish confession of faith for the London refugees. He belonged to a Sevillian circle influenced by Erasmian humanism, the reading of Castellio (opposing coercion in religion), and contacts with Franciscans and "spirituali" in Italy (his network included Italians; cf. the PIMo project). *verificar fuente*: Kinder (1975), ch. 8; Doris Moreno, *Casiodoro de Reina. Libertad y tolerancia en la Europa del siglo XVI* (2017); PIMo WG2 workshop (2023).
- **Lèse-majesté against Philip II:** The verifiable point is that Philip II **persecuted** him — a price on his head, spies, and the royal government's pressure for extradition. Whether the charges against him ever amounted to a legal conviction for *crimen laesae majestatis* (treason against the crown) is **not** established by the secondary literature; no such verdict is recorded. What **is** recorded is that the word was used by enemies and later chroniclers (e.g., the Venice/Ghent diplomatic correspondence, and later Menéndez Pelayo) to describe his circulation of heretical books. *verificar fuente*: Kinder (1975); *Historia de los heterodoxos españoles*, Libro IV; the letter of the Governor of Flanders to the regent of the Netherlands (as quoted by *protestantes.net*); legal-history literature on *laesae majestatis* (Remco, 26_01_EN_Trabajo_Mireya_Toribio.pdf).

## 6. Recommended primary sources for `verificar fuente`

1. **Archivo Histórico Nacional (AHN), Madrid** — *Procesos del Santo Oficio de Sevilla* concerning San Isidoro del Campo (late 1550s–1562). Especially the *auto-da-fé* record of 26 April 1562 and the testimony of María de Bohorques.
2. **Cartas de Casiodoro de Reina** (Sevilla: CIMPE, 2010?), ed. Andrés Oyola Fabián et al. — includes the letter of 9 January 1582 (terminus ante quem).
3. **Menéndez Pelayo, Marcelino**, *Historia de los heterodoxos españoles*, Vol. IV, capítulos 3–6 (as cited in *teologoresponde.org*).
4. **Kinder, A. Gordon**, *Casiodoro de Reina: Spanish Reformer of the Sixteenth Century* (London: Tamesis, 1975), esp. ch. 8 ("Theology and learning," pp. 82–92), and the biography of the German clergyman Boehmer (1879) on the Frankfurt pastorate.
5. **Doris Moreno**, *Casiodoro de Reina. Libertad y tolerancia en la Europa del siglo XVI* (Seville: Fundación Pública Andaluza de Cultura, 2017).
6. **PIMo, "Spaniards, Italians, and Reformers in Early Modern Europe"** — workshop proceedings and newsletter (2022–2023), for the wider Italian context in which Reina's exile is situated.

## 7. Summary of verdict

- **Exile from Spain after a 1582 Inquisition investigation:** the documented case is 1557 flight → late-1550s investigation → April 1562 *auto-da-fé* / *heresiarch* / Index. A **1582** investigation is **not** recorded; `verificar fuente` to the AHN process files and the final letter of 9 January 1582.
- **Time in Italy (Bologna, Rome):** **not recorded** in any standard biography. It is likely a conflation of Reina's Italian network (with Enzinas, Castellio, Ochino, Aconcio) or of Juan de Valdés (who was in Bologna/Rome/Naples, but that's a different person). `verificar fuente` to the PIMo archive and the AHN.
- **Theological stance / lèse-majesté:** Protestant, irenical, accused of heterodoxy; a real risk of royal pardon/capture from Philip II (price on the head, spies) is **verified**, but a legal conviction for *crimen laesae majestatis* is **not** documented. `verificar fuente`: Kinder (1975), §"Theology and learning"; the Flanders–Governor letter.
- **Return to Spain under Philip III:** **not verified** — Reina died in Frankfurt on 15 March 1594, four years before Philip III's accession. `verificar fuente`: the epitaph/portrait inscription and the standard biographies cited above.
